Automating a Broken Process Just Breaks It Faster

The most expensive automation mistake isn't the wrong tool — it's automating before validating the logic by hand. A field-tested checklist of what to stress-test before you touch a workflow builder.

Aaron Suarez May 2026 7 min read

"We should automate that."

I hear this at least twice a week. Usually from someone staring at a process that's already failing — tasks slipping, data inconsistent, handoffs ambiguous.

Their logic: automation will fix the inconsistency. It will not. Automation will make the inconsistency faster, cheaper, and harder to detect.

Here's the rule: validate by hand first. Automate second. Never reverse.

Should you automate this process?
↓
Step 1: Run it manually 5-10 times
↓
Does it produce consistent results every time?
✅ YES → Ready for automation
❌ NO → STOP. Fix the process first.
⚠️ If you automate an inconsistent process, you're just manufacturing errors at scale.

Automation doesn't solve logic problems. It amplifies them. If your process fails 10% of the time manually, it will fail 10% of the time automatically — but now it's failing 1,000 times a month instead of 10.

The Five Validation Questions (Answer Before You Build)

Before you open Make, Zapier, or any workflow builder, run your process through this checklist. Be honest. The answer protects your future self.

Question 1
Can you document every step?

If you can't write it down clearly, a machine won't understand it either.

Question 2
Does it have exceptions?

Exception-driven processes are terrible automation candidates. Handle the 90% case first. Automate exceptions separately or manually.

Question 3
Who owns each handoff?

Ambiguous ownership = automation that drops tasks into a void. Define "what happens if person X is unavailable" before you build.

Question 4
What's the failure mode?

When something breaks, does the automation alert someone or fail silently? Silent failure is the most dangerous outcome.

REAL EXAMPLE

A client automated their lead routing. When a lead came in, the system assigned it to the rep with the lowest current load. Great in theory. But one rep went on vacation and didn't mark themselves unavailable. The automation kept assigning leads to them. For two weeks. Silent failure. 47 leads, zero follow-up. The automation worked perfectly. The process was broken. They automated anyway.

The Validation Matrix

Run your process through this table. If you score more than one red, don't automate. Fix first.

Validation CriterionPass / FailNotes
Process runs without human judgment calls___If a person has to "decide" at any step, map those decisions first
Input data is structured and predictable___Free-text fields? Varied formats? Those need pre-processing

Why "Just Build It" Fails

The pressure to automate comes from good places: speed, consistency, scale. But the rush to build skips the most important step — testing the logic by hand.

Here's what happens when you skip validation:

  • Edge cases become emergencies. You didn't map what happens when a field is empty. Now 300 records have null values and your dashboard is broken.
  • Garbage multiplies. One bad data entry, manually, is a mistake. One bad data entry, automated, is 10,000 mistakes before lunch.
  • No one owns the errors. "The system did it" replaces accountability. But the system didn't fail — the person who built it without validation did.
THE 10-TEST RULE

Before you automate anything, run it manually 10 times. Not once. Not twice. Ten times. Record every exception, every weird input, every time someone had to "figure it out." That list of exceptions is your automation requirements doc. If you can't handle them all, don't automate yet.

The Right Order of Operations

Most teams do this: Build → Test → Deploy → (crash) → Debug → Fix process → Rebuild

That's expensive. Here's the correct order:

  1. Map — Document every step, decision, and handoff. Use a whiteboard. Get everyone in the room.
  2. Validate manually — Run the process as if the automation already exists. Someone pretends to be the "system." Do this 10-20 times. Note every failure.
  3. Fix the process — Adjust the steps based on what broke. Remove ambiguous decisions. Add missing handoffs. Simplify.
  4. Validate again — Run the fixed process manually another 10 times. If it fails even once, go back to step 3.
  5. Automate — Now you have a process that works consistently. Build the automation.
  6. Monitor — For the first month, check outputs daily. Compare to expected results. Silent failures kill.

The most expensive automation is the one that works perfectly — at producing the wrong outcome. Garbage in, gospel out. Speed doesn't fix logic. It just makes bad logic run faster.

A Real-World Example: Lead Intake

Here's a process I see automated prematurely all the time. The manual version has five steps:

  • Lead comes in via form → SDR reads and categorizes → Assigns to appropriate rep → Rep sends intro email → Lead is tagged in CRM

In manual mode, the SDR catches weird inputs: "I'm interested in your services" with no industry specified. They make a judgment call. Maybe they email back to ask. Maybe they assign to a generalist rep. The process works because a human fills the gaps.

Then someone automates it. The form field "industry" is optional. Now leads with no industry get assigned randomly. Or worse, they fall into a "default" queue that no one checks. The automation runs perfectly. The process is still broken. But now it's broken at scale.

The fix? Make "industry" required and validated on the form. Add a routing rule for "other." Build a manual review queue for anything that doesn't match. Those fixes should happen before automation, not after — but they almost never do.

BEFORE YOU AUTOMATE, ASK

What's the worst thing that could happen if this automation runs for a week without anyone checking it? If the answer makes you uncomfortable, you're not ready. Validate first. Automate second. Or pay for the lesson like everyone else does.

Automation is a force multiplier. That's exactly why it's dangerous. It multiplies what you already have — good or bad.

Most teams automate first and validate later. They learn expensive lessons about edge cases, data quality, and silent failure. Then they rebuild. Then they learn another lesson. Then they rebuild again.

The teams that get automation right spend 80% of their time on process validation and 20% on the build. Not the other way around.

Run it manually until it's boring. Then automate. Anything else is just manufacturing errors faster.
