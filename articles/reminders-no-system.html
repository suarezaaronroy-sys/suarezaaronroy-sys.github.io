If Your Team Needs Constant Reminders, You Don't Have a System

A system that requires a manager to remember to remind someone isn't a system — it's a dependency in disguise. The structural test that exposes which processes actually run themselves.

Aaron Suarez May 2026 6 min read

"Did you follow up with that lead?"

"Don't forget to update the CRM."

"Hey, just a reminder about the client call in an hour."

If these sentences are part of your daily vocabulary, you don't have a system. You have a supervision problem dressed up as management.

Real systems don't need reminders. They force action. They make the next step obvious, unavoidable, and visible. When you have to remind someone to do their job, the problem isn't their memory. It's your design.

Reminders are a symptom of system failure. Every time you remind someone to do something, you're revealing that the process doesn't work without you. That's not leadership. That's a crutch.

The Real Cost of Reminders

Most managers don't realize how expensive reminders actually are. It's not just the 30 seconds to type a message. It's everything else.

~15 hrs/week
Average manager time spent on reminders
(Based on audits of 20+ operations teams)

That's almost two full working days per week. Two days of your manager's time spent not managing — just remembering things for other people and telling them to do what they should already be doing.

Now multiply that by the number of people receiving reminders. Each interruption breaks their focus. Each "quick check-in" costs 10-15 minutes of context-switching recovery. The hidden tax is massive. And almost no one measures it.

THE REMINDER TAX FORMULA

(Time to send reminder × frequency) + (recipient recovery time × frequency) + (missed tasks when reminders are forgotten) = cost of broken system. For most teams, this adds up to 30-50 hours per week of wasted capacity. That's a full-time employee's worth of work. Buried in reminders.

Push vs. Pull: The Framework That Explains Everything

Here's the mental model that changed how I design processes.

🔴 PUSH SYSTEM (Bad)
Someone has to push information to the person who needs it. "I'll send you the report when it's ready." "Let me know when you finish that task."
Example: Manager reminds VA to update CRM
🟢 PULL SYSTEM (Good)
The information is available when and where it's needed. No one has to push. No one has to remind. The system surfaces what's needed automatically.
Example: Dashboard shows which leads need follow-up; VA checks it daily

Push systems require reminders. Pull systems don't. Every time you find yourself pushing information to someone, you've found a system that needs redesign.

Push systems scale poorly. The more people you add, the more reminders you need. Pull systems scale effortlessly. The system does the work. People just pull what they need when they need it.

The Dependency Audit

Here's a concrete exercise. List every process in your operation. For each one, ask: "Does this require a reminder to happen?"

Manager
Following up on leads
🔴 VA waits for "follow up with lead X"
VA
Updating CRM status
🔴 Manager says "update the deal stage"
Sales
Sending proposals
🔴 Admin reminds "proposal due tomorrow"

Each red line is a dependency. Each dependency is a failure point — because dependencies rely on human memory. And human memory fails constantly.

The goal isn't to eliminate all communication. The goal is to eliminate routine reminders. If you're reminding someone about something that happens more than once a week, that reminder should be automated or the process should be redesigned.

THE 3-REMINDER RULE

If you've reminded someone about the same task three times, stop. The problem isn't their memory. The problem is the system. Either the task isn't important (delete it), the trigger isn't clear (redesign it), or the ownership isn't assigned (fix it). Reminding them a fourth time won't help.

The System Test

Here's the most useful diagnostic tool I've found. It takes 30 seconds and reveals everything.

🔍 The Vasectomy Test (or "The Vacation Test")
If you left for two weeks — no calls, no emails, no reminders — would the process still happen correctly?

If the answer is no, you don't have a system. You have a dependency with a name attached.

Run this test on every critical process in your operation. The ones that fail are where you're spending your reminder energy. Those are also the ones you should fix first.

What to Do Instead of Reminding

You have three options when you find yourself about to send a reminder. Pick one.

  • Automate the trigger. Instead of "don't forget to follow up," set up a scheduled task or a CRM automation that surfaces leads needing attention. The system triggers, not you.
  • Make it visible. Instead of "update the CRM," put a dashboard on the wall (physical or digital) that shows stale records. Visibility creates accountability without nagging.
  • Assign ownership differently. If someone consistently needs reminders for a task, maybe they shouldn't own that task. Reassign it to someone who can handle it, or redesign it so it doesn't need ownership at all.

⚡ Quick win this week: Pick the process you send the most reminders about. Spend 30 minutes redesigning it so the next step is obvious without you. Test it for one week. Track how many reminders you send. The number will drop by at least 70%. You'll feel the difference immediately.

A Real Example: Lead Follow-Up

Before (Push System + Reminders): Lead comes in. Manager says "VA, follow up with this lead." VA does. Manager tracks in their head. After 3 days, manager asks "did you follow up?" VA says yes. But the follow-up didn't lead to a response. Now what? Another reminder to send a second email. The cycle repeats.

After (Pull System + No Reminders): Lead comes in. CRM creates a task automatically: "Email lead within 2 hours." Task appears on VA's dashboard. VA does it. Task moves to "waiting for reply." If no reply in 48 hours, a new task auto-creates: "Send follow-up." VA sees it. No manager involved. No reminders. Just a system that works.

The manager's job shifts from "reminding" to "designing the rules that make reminders unnecessary." That's the real work. That's also the work that scales.

Management is not reminding. Management is designing systems that don't need reminding. If you're spending your day sending reminders, you're not managing. You're being managed — by a broken system you haven't fixed.

The Blame Shift

Here's the part that makes people uncomfortable.

If you're constantly reminding someone, you probably think they're unreliable. But here's the truth: they're responding rationally to a bad design. They're not lazy. They're not forgetful. They're working in a system that doesn't surface priorities, doesn't make next steps obvious, and depends on you to function.

Blaming them for needing reminders is like blaming a thermostat for not working when you haven't set the temperature. The problem isn't the thermostat. It's the configuration. It's the design.

Stop blaming. Start designing.

THE HARD QUESTION

Ask yourself: if this person left tomorrow, would the process still work? If the answer depends on your reminders, the process is already broken. You're just the last person holding it together. That's not sustainable. That's not scalable. That's not a system.

Reminders are expensive. Not just in time — in trust, in focus, in morale. Every reminder is a tiny message that says "the system isn't working, so I have to step in."

The best teams don't need reminders. Their systems pull work forward. Dashboards show what needs attention. Automations trigger the next step. Ownership is clear. Visibility is automatic.

If you're still reminding people, you're not done building your system. Go back to the whiteboard. Redesign the process until the next step is obvious, visible, and unavoidable.

Then stop reminding. Watch what happens. The people you thought were unreliable will suddenly become reliable — because you finally gave them a system that works.
